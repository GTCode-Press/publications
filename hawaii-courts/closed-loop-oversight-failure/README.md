Hawaii's oversight bodies are often appointed by or work through the institutions they review. In this series, a "closed loop" means that appointments, information, and decisions remain close to the institution under scrutiny. Proceedings are often confidential, and reform bills can fail before changing the process.

The series compares judicial, executive, and police oversight. Records must establish why a complaint was closed, a prosecution declined, or a reform proposal failed. Can the public test the independence of a review when the institution under scrutiny controls appointments, information, or decisions? Ordinary explanations need to be tested against the records too. The arrangements themselves do not establish coordination between branches.

| | **Judicial** | **Executive** | **Law Enforcement** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Oversight body** | Commission on Judicial Conduct | Attorney General / SIPD | Police Commission / SHOPO |
| **Appointed by** | Supreme Court (all 7 members) | Governor | Mayor (7 members) |
| **Track record** | FY24–25: 11 processed / 6 dismissed / 5 pending | [July 24, 2026: Luke and four others indicted in the AG/SIPD case](https://www.civilbeat.org/2026/07/bribery-charges-sylvia-luke-ex-state-rep-and-others-indicted-in-corruption-case/); charges are not findings of guilt | ~75% of fired officers reinstated via arbitration |
| **Legislation and contracts** | HB 3056 (2008) — did not advance out of committee | SB2107 (2024) — passage recommended by Judiciary and Ways and Means despite AG opposition; not enacted | [2025–2029 compensation award issued through September 2025 arbitration](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/local-news/2025-11-20/police-across-the-state-set-to-receive-largest-raises-in-nearly-2-decades); county approvals followed. Current discipline terms not verified |
| **Confidentiality** | Rule 8.4: complaint-stage confidentiality, with specified public disclosures if formal discipline begins | Department generally does not confirm or deny investigations | Arbitration proceedings private |

## Part I: The Zero Commission
### The Judicial Branch

Seven members, all appointed by the Supreme Court. In FY 2024–2025 the Commission reported 1,355 inquiries, 11 complaints processed, 6 dismissed, and 5 pending at year-end. Proceedings remain confidential enough that the public cannot reconstruct the merits or screening path from the annual totals alone.

## Part II: The Paper Bag and the Architecture of Self-Investigation
### The Executive Branch

The Attorney General’s office opposed SB2107 in 2024 as unnecessary. [Judiciary](https://data.capitol.hawaii.gov/sessions/session2024/commreports/SB2107_SSCR2123_.HTM) and [Ways and Means](https://data.capitol.hawaii.gov/sessions/session2024/commreports/SB2107_SD1_SSCR3050_.HTM) nevertheless recommended passage; the bill was not enacted. In 2026, asked to investigate a senior elected official in the same executive administration, Lopez said no fully independent special-prosecutor process existed. SB2107 would have allowed outside counsel but retained AG oversight. The [July 24 indictment](https://www.civilbeat.org/2026/07/bribery-charges-sylvia-luke-ex-state-rep-and-others-indicted-in-corruption-case/) of Luke and four others contradicts any claim that this arrangement necessarily prevents charges against senior officials. Luke and three co-defendants [pleaded not guilty on July 30](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/text/local-news/2026-07-30/luke-others-plead-not-guilty-to-charges-in-bribery-scandal); Solidum did not appear. The charges neither establish the original $35,000 paper-bag allegation nor resolve whether an arm’s-length review would provide greater independence or public confidence. *Amemiya v. Sapienza* supplies a public-trust standard for evaluating disqualification; the different appointment relationships require assessment on their own facts.

## Records That Would Clarify the Loop

The series turns on reviewability. The most useful next records are ordinary: CJC recusal and disposition statistics that do not identify complainants; SIPD case, staffing, and disposition reports of the kind proposed in unenacted SB2930 SD2; written conflict-screening analysis for high-level executive-branch investigations; Police Commission complaint-outcome summaries; SHOPO arbitration outcomes in machine-readable form; and any legislative files explaining why reform bills did not advance. Those records could explain how oversight decisions were made without requiring any reader to infer motive from silence.
